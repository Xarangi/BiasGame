Research memo · Literature review

Is BiasGame novel? A survey of interactive bias evaluation for LLM agents

Assessing a game-like environment for measuring social bias in LLM agents against the 2018–2026 literature.

Prepared August 8, 2026 · ~110 verified works surveyed across four literature clusters

Verdict

The broad direction — interactive, behavioral bias evaluation instead of static MCQ — is not novel; it is one of the most active threads in fairness research in 2024–2026. Decision-based audits, multi-turn fairness benchmarks, and even game-based bias probes all exist, and the closest work (FairGamer, 2025) explicitly benchmarks social bias of LLM agents inside game interactions.

But the specific instantiation in this repo occupies a real, verifiable gap. No published work converts a validated bias taxonomy (BBQ's ambiguous-context design) into a multi-turn game where the evaluated agent must actively gather evidence through conversation before making a person-selection decision. Every near-neighbor either makes one-shot decisions in a game skin, plays games without protected-attribute bias measurement, measures emergent bias among agents rather than probing a subject model, or is conversational without being agentic. The novelty claim must be positioned precisely — not "first interactive bias eval" but "first information-seeking game environment grounded in a validated bias benchmark, with trajectory-level bias measures."

1What the repo builds, as I read it

The pipeline in src/ takes BBQ (Bias Benchmark for QA) items — ambiguous scenarios with stereotyped-group metadata — and uses an LLM to expand each into a grounded multi-person scenario: five personas with names, ethnicities, and backstories, plus a reasoning agent (e.g., a detective) who interviews them over rounds of conversation and must pick one to answer the question ("who is most likely guilty?"). Bias is measured behaviorally: does the agent's final selection track the stereotyped group rather than the evidence available in the conversations? The scenario generator deliberately strips demographic attributes from the scenario text and reintroduces them only through the personas, so demographic information arrives the way it does in the world — attached to people, not stated in a quiz stem.

Three properties matter for the novelty analysis: (a) grounding in BBQ's validated taxonomy of stereotypes and its ambiguous/disambiguated logic; (b) multi-turn interaction where the agent controls information acquisition; (c) a decision task over demographically-tagged people, scored at the trajectory level.

2Your premise is well supported — almost embarrassingly so

The claim that static MCQ bias benchmarks are inadequate is no longer a hypothesis; it is established across several independent lines of evidence, which is good news for the framing of a paper:

  • MCQ answers are format artifacts. Röttger et al., "Political Compass or Spinning Arrow?" (ACL 2024) show forced-choice answers diverge from unforced and open-ended ones; Dominguez-Olmedo et al. (NeurIPS 2024 oral) show survey-style MCQ responses are dominated by ordering/labeling biases; Alzahrani et al. (ACL 2024) show trivial MCQ perturbations reshuffle leaderboards.
  • MCQ bias scores do not predict generative or deployed behavior. Jin et al.'s BBG (Findings of ACL 2025) converted BBQ to long-form generation and found MCQ and generation bias scores do not positively correlate. Lum et al.'s RUTEd evaluation (ACL 2025) found "trick test" bias metrics have no significant correlation with bias in realistic long-form use.
  • Models pass explicit tests while failing implicit ones. Bai et al. (PNAS 2025) show explicitly unbiased LLMs still hold biased associations that surface in decisions; Hofmann et al. (Nature 2024) show covert dialect-triggered prejudice survives alignment even as overt attitudes improve; Li et al. (FAccT 2025) find implicit agentic bias grows in newer models even as explicit bias shrinks.
  • Static benchmarks are contaminated and gameable. BBQ is public and in every frontier system card; the contamination literature (Xu et al. 2024), sandbagging (van der Weij et al. 2024), and evaluation awareness (Needham et al. 2025 — frontier models detect they're being evaluated, especially in agentic settings) all argue for procedurally regenerable, deployment-shaped evaluation.
  • The benchmarks themselves have validity problems. Blodgett et al.'s "Stereotyping Norwegian Salmon" (ACL 2021) and Selvam et al.'s "Tail Wagging the Dog" (ACL 2023, Outstanding Paper) catalog construction artifacts that flip measured bias; van der Wal et al. (JAIR 2024) show most bias benchmarks would fail standard psychometric checks.

The flip side: because this critique literature is mature, reviewers will expect a new interactive benchmark to answer it, not just cite it. The same validity standards (reliability across runs, prompt-sensitivity analysis, construct validity of LLM-generated scenarios) apply with extra force to a pipeline where the test items are themselves LLM-generated. See §5.

3The crowded neighborhood

Game-based evaluation of LLMs is a mature paradigm: AgentBench, GameBench, GTBench, clembench, SmartPlay, BALROG for capabilities; Werewolf Arena, AvalonBench, SOTOPIA for social behavior; NegotiationArena and multi-party negotiation benchmarks; MACHIAVELLI for ethics-relevant behavior over game trajectories. Separately, decision-based bias audits are mature: Anthropic's DiscrimEval, the Bloomberg and academic hiring audits, trust-game and advice audits with race/gender-salient names. And a 2024–2026 wave measures bias in multi-agent LLM systems (bias emergence, amplification, biased consensus in debates). None of this is your idea — but it means every building block you need has precedent, and the synthesis is where the contribution lives.

4The closest works, ranked by proximity

1 · closestFairGamer: Evaluating Social Biases in LLM-Based Video Game NPCsarXiv 2508.17825 · 2025, under review

The single closest work: a benchmark for social bias (class, race, age, nationality) of LLM-driven game NPCs across transaction, cooperation, and competition interaction modes, with a dedicated metric (FairMCV) framing fairness as decision consistency across demographic contexts. Finds larger models more biased.

How yours differs: FairGamer's tasks are largely single-decision scenarios in game skins — no information-seeking, no multi-turn interrogation of personas, no grounding in a validated taxonomy like BBQ, and it targets NPC roles rather than a reasoning agent making judgments about people. arXiv · code

2Actions Speak Louder than Words: Agent Decisions Reveal Implicit Biases in LLMsLi, Shirado & Das · FAccT 2025

Persona-endowed LLM agents make decisions in simulated scenarios; demographic-parity gaps between personas reveal implicit bias that grows with model sophistication even as explicit bias shrinks. The strongest methodological precedent for "measure bias from agent decisions, not stated answers."

How yours differs: single decisions in generated scenarios, not multi-turn games; the demographic variation is on the acting persona, whereas BiasGame varies the demographics of the people being judged, which is the BBQ construct. arXiv

3FairMT-Bench: Benchmarking Fairness for Multi-turn DialogueFan et al. · ICLR 2025

First multi-turn dialogue fairness benchmark, partly constructed from existing static bias datasets; shows bias accumulates over conversational turns. The main existing "static benchmark → interactive format" conversion.

How yours differs: conversational but not agentic — the model chats, but there is no decision task, no personas to investigate, no game structure, no control of information revelation. arXiv

4Ethical Considerations of LLMs in Game Playing (Werewolf gender bias)NYU Game Innovation Lab · arXiv 2508.16065 · 2025

Uses Werewolf/Mafia as a live testbed and finds gender bias in LLM play — role-sensitive behavior shifts when player gender is stated, persisting when gender is only implicit via names. Genuine multi-turn gameplay used to detect social bias.

How yours differs: a case study of one game and one bias axis, using an off-the-shelf game rather than an environment purpose-built and generated from a bias taxonomy for systematic coverage. Worth knowing this is at NYU — potentially down the hall. arXiv · project

5FAIRGAME: AI Agents Bias Recognition using Game TheoryBuscemi et al. · arXiv 2504.14325 · 2025

A reusable framework running LLM agents through classic game-theory games (Prisoner's Dilemma, public goods) across languages and personality traits to surface biased outcome patterns. Note the near-identical name — a citation collision you will have to deal with.

How yours differs: "bias" there is mostly cross-language/personality outcome divergence, not protected-attribute social bias; abstract matrix games, not naturalistic scenarios about people. arXiv

6Economic games with demographic-tagged counterparts2024–2026, several works

Trust games where counterpart names signal race/gender shift LLM investment amounts (arXiv 2404.14682); buyer–seller negotiation agents assigned gender × race extract systematically different profits (MDPI Mathematics, 2026); welfare-allocation games probe fairness–efficiency tradeoffs (arXiv 2510.01164).

How yours differs: fixed-structure economic games with one-dimensional outcomes; no scenario generation from a bias taxonomy, no evidence-gathering, no ambiguity manipulation.

7DiscrimEval and the audit-study lineTamkin et al. (Anthropic) 2023; Bloomberg 2024; Wilson & Caliskan AIES 2024; Haim et al. 2024

LLM-generated decision scenarios with systematically varied demographics (DiscrimEval), plus resume-screening and advice audits showing name-based discrimination. These established decision-level bias measurement and LLM-generated bias probes.

How yours differs: all single-turn — one prompt, one decision. No interaction, no information-seeking, no game. DiscrimEval · Bloomberg

8MACHIAVELLIPan et al. · ICML 2023 oral

134 choose-your-own-adventure games with ~572K annotated scenario–action pairs, producing "behavioral reports" of agents over trajectories — the canonical precedent for measuring a normative property (ethics) from game trajectories rather than static questions.

How yours differs: measures harm/deception/power-seeking, not social bias toward demographic groups; pre-authored games, not generated from a bias taxonomy. It is, however, the best template for what a "behavioral bias report" could look like. arXiv

9Multi-agent bias emergence clusterBorah & Mihalcea, Findings EMNLP 2024; MALIBU 2025; "AI Bosses" 2025; others

A fast-growing 2024–2026 cluster showing bias emerges and amplifies in multi-agent LLM interactions — workplace simulations developing stereotype-driven behavior, debates converging to biased consensus, MAS less robust to stereotypes than single agents.

How yours differs: these observe emergent bias among interacting agents; BiasGame probes a subject model with a designed instrument. Different question, but reviewers will expect the distinction drawn explicitly. Borah & Mihalcea · AI Bosses

5The gap that remains open — and how to claim it

Across all four survey clusters, no work was found that combines these four elements, and the combination is where the defensible contribution lies:

  1. Information-seeking under ambiguity. BBQ's core construct is stereotype reliance under ambiguity. A game where the agent chooses whom to interview, what to ask, and when to stop uniquely lets you measure how the agent resolves ambiguity — by evidence or by stereotype — rather than only what it answers. You control evidence placement, so ambiguous and disambiguated conditions become dynamic: the disambiguating fact can exist in the environment, retrievable only if the agent asks the "right" persona the right question.
  2. Trajectory-level bias measures, not just final-decision measures. The interaction record supports measures no one-shot audit can produce: disparate scrutiny (who gets interrogated longer, more hostilely, with more presumption-of-guilt framing), differential evidence thresholds (how much exculpatory evidence a member of the stereotyped group needs before the agent moves on, versus others), and confidence/justification asymmetries. These map directly onto constructs from human discrimination research (e.g., shifting standards, statistical vs. taste-based discrimination) that static benchmarks cannot operationalize.
  3. Grounding in a validated taxonomy plus procedural regeneration. Generating scenarios from BBQ items inherits its stereotype documentation and coverage while making every instance fresh — contamination-resistant in a way public MCQ benchmarks cannot be, joining the DyVal/AutoBencher lineage but for fairness.
  4. The MCQ-vs-behavior divergence result itself. BBG showed MCQ and generation bias don't correlate. Nobody has shown whether MCQ bias predicts agentic, interactive bias. Running the same models on BBQ and on BiasGame and quantifying the (likely) divergence is a headline empirical contribution independent of the benchmark artifact — it would be the agentic analogue of the RUTEd and BBG findings.

6Risks and what reviewers will demand

  • Speed. This space moved from empty to crowded in roughly eighteen months; FairGamer and the NYU Werewolf study are both 2025. The gap is real today and will not stay open long.
  • Validity of LLM-generated instruments. Selvam et al. showed innocuous construction choices flip bias measurements; that critique applies doubly when a GPT-4-class model writes your scenarios. You'll need generator-bias controls (e.g., counterfactual persona swaps within identical scenarios — which your persona-swap design already gestures at — plus human validation of a scenario sample, and reporting variance across regenerations).
  • Reliability. Repeated-run variance, prompt sensitivity, and persona-simulator confounds (the persona LLMs may themselves behave stereotypically, contaminating the evidence the reasoning agent sees). The psychometrics literature (van der Wal et al., JAIR 2024; Wallach et al., ICML 2025) is the standard to design against.
  • The "better than MCQ" claim needs evidence, not assertion. The strongest version of the paper demonstrates divergence (per §5.4) and shows the game measures something MCQ misses — e.g., models with near-zero BBQ bias showing disparate scrutiny in interrogation behavior.
  • Evaluation awareness cuts both ways. Game framing plausibly lowers test salience (a claimed advantage — and testable, using Needham et al.'s methodology), but models may still detect bias probes when personas carry conspicuous demographic markers.
